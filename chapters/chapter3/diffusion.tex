
\section{Diffusion and Flow-Matching Models}
\label{sec:diffusion}

Diffusion models and flow-matching models are the current state of the art in generative modeling, having displaced GANs, VAEs, and normalizing flows on most high-fidelity generation benchmarks.
Both can be understood as improvements that address the core limitations identified in the previous section: the training instability of GANs, the Gaussian mean-prediction bias of VAEs, and the architectural constraints of normalizing flows.
The key idea shared by both families is to decompose a complex single-step transformation between a noise distribution and the data distribution into many small, tractable steps, allowing the model to be both highly expressive and stably trained.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models}
\label{sec:ddpm}

Diffusion models~\cite{Ho:2020ddpm,TODO:sohl_dickstein} frame generation as the reversal of a \textit{forward process} that progressively destroys the structure of a data sample by adding Gaussian noise.
The forward process is a fixed (not learned) Markov chain over $T$ discrete time steps:
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\right),
    \label{eqn:forward_step}
\end{equation}
where $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$ is a fixed \textit{noise schedule} with $0 < \beta_t \ll 1$.
Each step mixes the previous sample toward zero mean while adding a small amount of isotropic Gaussian noise.
The joint forward distribution over the full chain is
\begin{equation}
    q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}).
    \label{eqn:forward_joint}
\end{equation}
By choosing the noise schedule so that $\prod_{t=1}^T (1 - \beta_t) \to 0$, the marginal $q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ for sufficiently large $T$: the data is destroyed into pure noise.

A useful closed form for the marginal at arbitrary $t$ follows from defining $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$:
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\right).
    \label{eqn:forward_marginal}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, a noisy sample at step $t$ can be written in closed form as
\begin{equation}
    x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
    \label{eqn:xt_reparam}
\end{equation}
This reparameterization is important: it allows a noisy training sample at any time step to be constructed directly from $x_0$ and a single noise draw, without simulating the full chain.

\subsubsection{The DDPM Objective as a Hierarchical ELBO}

The generative model learns the reverse process
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{t-1};\; \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t)\right),
    \label{eqn:reverse_step}
\end{equation}
which is parameterized by a neural network.
Starting from $p(x_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, iteratively applying \Cref{eqn:reverse_step} for $t = T, T-1, \ldots, 1$ produces a sample from the learned data distribution.
The model is trained by maximizing a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x_0)$.

The training objective has the same ELBO structure derived in \Cref{sec:vae}, but applied to a hierarchical latent variable model with $T$ latent variables $x_1, \ldots, x_T$:
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(x_0)
    &\geq \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)\right]
      - D_\text{KL}\!\left(q(x_T \mid x_0) \;\|\; p(x_T)\right) \notag \\
    &\quad - \sum_{t=2}^{T} \underbrace{D_\text{KL}\!\left(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \;\|\; p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\right)}_{L_{t-1}}.
    \label{eqn:ddpm_elbo}
\end{align}
The first term is a reconstruction term at $t=1$; the second is a boundary term that is negligible when the schedule is well-chosen; the sum over $L_{t-1}$ is the main contribution.
Each $L_{t-1}$ compares the learned reverse step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ against the \textit{fixed} forward-process posterior $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, which is tractable because the forward process is Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
    q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)
    = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{t-1};\; \tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_t I\right),
    \label{eqn:forward_posterior}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0)
    = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,x_0
      + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,x_t,
    \qquad
    \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t.
    \label{eqn:posterior_mean}
\end{equation}
Crucially, the posterior \Cref{eqn:forward_posterior} is \textit{fixed} and Gaussian: the diffusion model replaces the learned approximate posterior of the VAE with a fixed stochastic process.
Each term $L_{t-1}$ is the KL divergence between two Gaussians and reduces to a squared difference of their means.

\subsubsection{The Noise Prediction Objective}

Substituting \Cref{eqn:xt_reparam} into the posterior mean \Cref{eqn:posterior_mean} and parameterizing the reverse process mean as
\begin{equation}
    \mu_\theta(x_t, t)
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right),
    \label{eqn:reverse_mean_reparam}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_\theta$ is a neural network that predicts the noise $\varepsilon$, each KL term $L_{t-1}$ simplifies to
\begin{equation}
    L_{t-1} \propto \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,\varepsilon}\!\left[\left\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\; t\right)\right\|^2\right].
    \label{eqn:ddpm_loss}
\end{equation}
The full training objective is therefore a sum of MSE losses, each comparing the network's predicted noise to the true noise injected at time step $t$.
In practice, Ho et al.~\cite{Ho:2020ddpm} found that uniform random sampling of $t$ at each training step, rather than summing all terms, works well and further simplifies training.
The resulting algorithm is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sample $x_0 \sim p_\text{data}$, $t \sim \text{Uniform}(1, T)$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
    \item Construct $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$ via \Cref{eqn:xt_reparam}.
    \item Take a gradient step to minimize $\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2$.
\end{enumerate}
The overall structure of the forward and reverse processes is illustrated in \Cref{fig:ddpm}.

\subsubsection{Expressivity and the Removal of the Gaussian Mean Bias}

A key conceptual advantage of diffusion models over VAEs is that the reverse process is not required to predict the mean of the full posterior $p_\text{data}(x_0 \mid x_T)$ in a single step.
Instead it predicts the mean of the posterior $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ at each small step, which by construction (\Cref{eqn:forward_posterior}) is unimodal and Gaussian.
Over many steps, the composition of these simple Gaussian steps can produce samples from a highly multimodal distribution.
The Gaussian mean-prediction bias identified in the VAE discussion is therefore not a fundamental limitation of diffusion models, which is part of the theoretical justification for their superior sample quality.

\subsubsection{Sampling via Reverse-Process SDEs}

To generate a new sample, one draws $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and iterates the learned reverse step for $t = T, \ldots, 1$.
This sequential sampling procedure is the main practical cost of diffusion models: generating a single sample requires $T$ (typically hundreds to thousands) forward passes through the network, compared to one for a normalizing flow or GAN.
A key theoretical insight is that the forward process in \Cref{eqn:forward_step} can be interpreted as the discretization of a \textit{stochastic differential equation} (SDE)~\cite{Song:2021score}.
The SDE perspective reveals that every forward SDE has a corresponding \textit{probability flow ODE} with identical marginal distributions $q(x_t)$ at every time $t$:
\begin{equation}
    dx = \left[f(x, t) - \tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log q_t(x)\right] dt,
    \label{eqn:prob_flow_ode}
\end{equation}
where $f$ and $g$ are the drift and diffusion coefficients of the forward SDE and $\nabla_x \log q_t(x)$ is the \textit{score function} of the marginal distribution at time $t$.
Since $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \approx -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_{x_t} \log q_t(x_t)$, a trained diffusion model can solve either the stochastic reverse SDE or the deterministic probability flow ODE.
The ODE solution, which requires fewer function evaluations, is the continuous-time generalization of normalizing flows~\cite{TODO:chen_neuralode}.
This connection shows that diffusion models subsume normalizing flows as a special case and inherit their exact likelihood computation, while lifting the bijectivity constraint that limited flow architectures.

\subsection{Flow Matching}
\label{sec:flow_matching}

Flow matching~\cite{lipman2022flow} is a closely related but conceptually distinct framework that trains a model to directly predict the \textit{vector field} that transports a noise distribution to the data distribution, rather than predicting added noise.
The starting point is the \textit{continuity equation}: if $p_t(x)$ is a time-dependent density evolving under the vector field $v_t(x)$, then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (p_t\,v_t) = 0.
    \label{eqn:continuity}
\end{equation}
Given a source distribution $p_0 = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a target $p_1 = p_\text{data}$, the goal is to learn a neural network $u_\theta(x, t)$ that approximates the vector field $v_t$ generating a probability path $\{p_t\}_{t \in [0,1]}$ connecting them.

The marginal vector field $v_t(x)$ is generally intractable to compute directly.
The key insight of conditional flow matching (CFM) is that the \textit{conditional} vector field $u_t(x \mid x_1)$, which generates the conditional path $p_t(x \mid x_1)$ for a fixed data point $x_1$, has the same expected gradient and yields an equivalent training objective~\cite{lipman2022flow}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{CFM}(\theta)
    = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_1 \sim p_\text{data},\,x_t \sim p_t(\cdot \mid x_1)}
      \left\| u_\theta(x_t, t) - u_t(x_t \mid x_1) \right\|^2.
    \label{eqn:cfm_loss}
\end{equation}
The conditional vector field $u_t(x \mid x_1)$ depends on the choice of conditional probability path.
With Gaussian conditional paths $p_t(x \mid x_1) = \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_t(x_1),\, \sigma_t^2 I)$, the CFM objective is equivalent to the diffusion score-matching objective~\cite{lipman2022flow}.

A practically important alternative is to use \textit{linear} (optimal transport) paths, defined by the straight interpolation
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
    \label{eqn:linear_path}
\end{equation}
for which the conditional vector field is simply $u_t(x \mid x_1) = x_1 - x_0$.
These straight paths are shorter on average than the curved trajectories traced by diffusion SDEs, which means fewer integration steps are needed to generate a sample accurately.
\Cref{fig:flow_matching} illustrates the contrast between diffusion trajectories and linear flow-matching paths.

\subsection{Applications in HEP and Comparative Performance}
\label{sec:diffusion_hep}

Diffusion models were adopted quickly within HEP after they demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on image generation tasks.
The first applications targeted fast calorimeter shower simulation~\cite{Mikuni:2022xry,Amram:2023onf}, replacing the computationally expensive \textsc{Geant4} full simulation~\cite{GEANT4:2002zbu} with a generative model that achieves comparable fidelity in a fraction of the time.
Diffusion and flow-matching models have since been applied to anomaly detection and unbinned unfolding, the latter being a central topic of \Cref{ch:unfolding}.

The Calorimeter Simulation Challenge (CaloChallenge)~\cite{Krause:2024avx} provides a systematic community benchmark for comparing generative models on calorimeter simulation across datasets of increasing dimensionality and complexity.
Results from this challenge are shown in \Cref{fig:calochallenge_ds2,fig:calochallenge_ds3}.
Conditional flow matching models achieve the best overall sample fidelity on these benchmarks, but normalizing-flow-based methods~\cite{Schnake:2024mip} are competitive and substantially faster to sample from, given their single-pass inference.

Beyond calorimeter simulation, there is growing evidence that CFM objectives outperform diffusion on HEP generative tasks more broadly~\cite{Favaro:2024rle,Petitjean:2025tgk}, likely because straighter probability paths require fewer integration steps and are less susceptible to discretization error.
An explicit comparison of diffusion and flow-matching objectives in the context of unbinned unfolding will be presented in \Cref{ch:unfolding}.
