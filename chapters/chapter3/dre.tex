
\section{Density Ratio Estimation}
\label{sec:dre}

The generative models described in the previous two sections model $p_\text{data}(x)$ directly.
This section introduces a complementary approach that sidesteps direct density modeling entirely and instead estimates the \textit{ratio} of two densities.
Density ratio estimation turns out to be a considerably simpler problem than density estimation, and the methods described here are used extensively in deep learning applications in HEP.
They are also central to the Omnifold unfolding method discussed in \Cref{ch:unfolding}, which is used for the cross-section measurement presented in \Cref{ch:zjets}.

\subsection{The Likelihood Ratio Trick}
\label{sec:lrt}

Given two probability densities $p(x)$ and $q(x)$, the likelihood ratio is defined as
\begin{equation}
    r(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
    \label{eqn:likelihood_ratio}
\end{equation}
The likelihood ratio trick is a method for computing $r(x)$ that only requires training a neural network classifier with binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss\footnote{Other loss functions such as the MSE and MLC losses are also minimized by the likelihood ratio and so can be used in this way as well.}.
This is a much simpler problem than density esimtation which attempts to model $p(x)$ or $q(x)$ directly.

\subsubsection{Proof: the Optimal BCE Classifier Recovers the Likelihood Ratio}

Consider a classifier $f_\theta(x) \in (0,1)$ trained on a balanced dataset of samples from $p(x)$ (label $y=1$) and $q(x)$ (label $y=0$) using the BCE loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(f) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log f(x)\right]
                    -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\!\left[\log(1 - f(x))\right].
    \label{eqn:bce_dre}
\end{equation}
We seek the function $f^*$ that minimizes this loss over all measurable functions.
Writing the expectations as integrals gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(f) = -\int \!\left[p(x)\log f(x) + q(x)\log(1-f(x))\right] dx.
    \label{eqn:bce_integral}
\end{equation}
To find the minimizer we take the functional derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $f$.
Perturbing $f \to f + \epsilon\,\eta$ for an arbitrary test function $\eta$, the two logarithms in the integrand expand to first order in $\epsilon$ as
\begin{align}
    \log\!\left(f(x) + \epsilon\,\eta(x)\right)
        &= \log f(x) + \frac{\epsilon\,\eta(x)}{f(x)} + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2), \label{eqn:log_expand1}\\
    \log\!\left(1 - f(x) - \epsilon\,\eta(x)\right)
        &= \log(1-f(x)) - \frac{\epsilon\,\eta(x)}{1-f(x)} + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2). \label{eqn:log_expand2}
\end{align}
Substituting into $\mathcal{L}(f+\epsilon\,\eta)$ and collecting terms by order in $\epsilon$,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(f + \epsilon\,\eta)
    &= -\int\!\left[p(x)\left(\log f(x) + \frac{\epsilon\,\eta(x)}{f(x)}\right)
       + q(x)\left(\log(1-f(x)) - \frac{\epsilon\,\eta(x)}{1-f(x)}\right)\right]dx
       + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \notag\\
    &= \mathcal{L}(f)
       - \epsilon\int\!\left[\frac{p(x)}{f(x)} - \frac{q(x)}{1-f(x)}\right]\eta(x)\,dx
       + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2).
    \label{eqn:functional_perturb}
\end{align}
The functional derivative is read off as the coefficient of $\eta(x)$ in the first-order term:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \mathcal{L}}{\delta f(x)} = -\frac{p(x)}{f(x)} + \frac{q(x)}{1-f(x)}.
    \label{eqn:functional_deriv}
\end{equation}
Setting $\delta\mathcal{L}/\delta f(x) = 0$ for all $x$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{p(x)}{f^*(x)} = \frac{q(x)}{1-f^*(x)}
    \;\implies\;
    f^*(x) = \frac{p(x)}{p(x) + q(x)}.
    \label{eqn:optimal_classifier}
\end{equation}
This is a global minimum: the second functional derivative $\delta^2\mathcal{L}/\delta f(x)^2 = p(x)/f(x)^2 + q(x)/(1-f(x))^2 > 0$ everywhere.
The likelihood ratio is then recovered from the optimal classifier output by a simple algebraic rearrangement:
\begin{equation}
    r(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{f^*(x)}{1 - f^*(x)}.
    \label{eqn:ratio_from_classifier}
\end{equation}
The optimal classifier output is therefore a monotonic function of the likelihood ratio.
Any classifier trained to minimize BCE loss is implicitly learning to estimate $r(x)$, up to this monotonic rescaling.
This result is sometimes called the \textit{likelihood ratio trick}~\cite{Cranmer:2015bka,Andreassen:2019nnm}.

\subsection{Reweighting and Applications in HEP}
\label{sec:dre_hep}

The practical consequence of \Cref{eqn:ratio_from_classifier} is that a trained classifier can be used to reweight a set of samples drawn from $q(x)$ to match the distribution $p(x)$.
Assigning each sample $x_i \sim q$ a weight $w_i = r(x_i) = f^*(x_i) / (1 - f^*(x_i))$ makes the weighted distribution equal to $p(x)$ in expectation.

Reweighting samples between probability distributions is not a new idea in HEP.
A standard method is to derive correction factors as ratios of one-dimensional histograms, which is a simple form of density ratio estimation in a single variable.
The limitation is evident: histogram-based reweighting does not generalize to more than a few dimensions before statistical power is exhausted.

The likelihood ratio trick bypasses this limitation entirely.
Because the classifier can be any neural network, it scales to high and even variable-dimensional inputs.
Graph networks and transformers, which naturally handle variable-length sets of particle four-momenta, can serve as the classifier backbone, making the method applicable to full event-level data without any binning or dimensionality reduction.
The method has been applied to Monte Carlo reweighting~\cite{Cranmer:2015bka}, where a simulation is reweighted to better match data, and to unbinned unfolding, where it is iterated to correct for detector effects.
The latter application, the Omnifold algorithm~\cite{Andreassen:2019cjw}, is the method used for the cross-section measurement in \Cref{ch:zjets}.
A concrete illustration of the quality of the reweighting in the first step of that procedure is given in \Cref{ch:zjets}.
