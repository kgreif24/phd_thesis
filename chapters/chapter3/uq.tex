
\section{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:uq}

Uncertainty quantification in the context of machine learning is not a single well-defined problem.
The relevant uncertainties and the appropriate methods for assessing them depend strongly on the application.
This section surveys the main categories of uncertainty that arise when deploying ML models, with particular attention to the cases most relevant in HEP.
\Cref{fig:uncertainty_types} illustrates each category on a toy problem.

\subsection{Aleatoric Uncertainty}
\label{sec:aleatoric}

Aleatoric uncertainty is the irreducible uncertainty inherent to the data-generating process itself.
No amount of additional data or a better model can eliminate it; it is a fundamental property of the problem.
A concrete example in HEP is jet classification.
A given jet configuration may have been produced by a top quark, a light quark, or a gluon, and in many cases the jet kinematics alone do not determine the answer unambiguously.
The correct model output in this case is not a hard classification but a probability, and the spread of that probability across classes reflects genuine physical ambiguity.
Reducing aleatoric uncertainty requires reframing the problem, for example by adding a new detector subsystem that provides additional discriminating information.
These uncertainties are therefore largely outside the scope of the ML methods discussed in this thesis, though a well-calibrated model should reflect them accurately in its output probabilities.

\subsection{Epistemic Uncertainty}
\label{sec:epistemic}

Epistemic uncertainty arises from limitations of the model rather than limitations of the data-generating process.
Sources include finite training data, limited model capacity, and optimization failures.
Unlike aleatoric uncertainty, epistemic uncertainty is in principle reducible by collecting more data, using a more expressive architecture, or improving training.
In HEP, the relevance of epistemic uncertainty depends heavily on the task.
For jet tagging, training datasets are large and the classifiers are highly expressive, so epistemic uncertainty in the model predictions is generally a small effect.
For applications such as unfolding, simulation-based inference (SBI), and anomaly detection, however, the model outputs are used directly as physical estimates, and epistemic uncertainty can have a significant impact on the result.

Several methods are used to estimate epistemic uncertainty in practice.

\paragraph{Ensembling.}
The simplest and most widely used method is to train multiple independent models and treat the spread of their outputs as an estimate of the model uncertainty.
Each model in the ensemble is initialized differently and may see a different order of training data, so differences in their predictions reflect genuine sensitivity to training stochasticity and finite-sample effects.
This approach has been applied in ATLAS measurements, for example in Ref.~\cite{ATLAS:2025clx}.
Ensembling provides a practical uncertainty estimate with relatively little overhead, but it is important to note that it captures only a subset of the full epistemic uncertainty: the spread of an ensemble trained on the same dataset cannot detect systematic biases common to all ensemble members.

\paragraph{Bayesian neural networks.}
A more principled approach is to place a prior distribution over the model weights and compute the posterior given the training data.
The posterior predictive distribution then naturally encodes epistemic uncertainty due to limited data.
In practice, exact Bayesian inference over neural network weights is intractable, and various approximations are used, such as variational inference or Monte Carlo dropout.
Bayesian neural networks have seen limited use in HEP~\cite{Bollweg:2019skg} relative to ensembling, in part because the approximations involved can be difficult to validate and the computational cost is higher.

\subsection{Domain Shift}
\label{sec:domain_shift}

Domain shift is a distinct category of uncertainty that occurs when the distribution of inputs at evaluation time differs from the distribution seen during training.
In HEP this is ubiquitous: ML models are almost always trained on simulation but applied to real collision data.
Mismodeling in the simulation introduces a systematic mismatch between the training and evaluation distributions, meaning the model may have learned a function of the input that is well-calibrated on simulation but not on data.
Domain shift is therefore closely related to the treatment of systematic uncertainties, which is a central concern in most physics measurements.
Several strategies are used to handle it.

\paragraph{Propagating uncertainties.}
The simplest strategy is to propagate the systematic uncertainty on the input $x$ through the trained model to obtain an uncertainty on $f(x)$.
This accounts for the effect of the domain shift on the model output in the region of input space where the model is evaluated, but ignores any effect the shift may have had on the training process itself.
If the model was trained in a region of input space that differs systematically from where it is evaluated, this approach will give a sub-optimal or incorrect result.
It is nonetheless widely used in practice given its simplicity, and is the approach taken in the jet tagging analysis described in \Cref{ch:tagging}.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation.}
A more sophisticated approach attempts to make the model explicitly robust to known sources of domain shift during training.
One example is decorrelating the model output against a nuisance parameter or other variable associated with the domain shift, so that systematic variations in that variable do not propagate to the output.
This has been studied in the context of jet taggers and other classifiers~\cite{Kasieczka:2020yyl,ATLAS:2025dkv,Ghosh:2021hrh}.
The advantage is a model that is more robust to the specific form of domain shift it was trained against.
The disadvantage is that imposing such constraints typically degrades performance on the primary task, introducing a tradeoff between robustness and discrimination power.

\paragraph{Parametrized models.}
The most complete approach is to incorporate knowledge of the domain shift directly into the model by conditioning on the nuisance parameter or systematic variation as an additional input~\cite{Louppe:2016ylz,Baldi:2016fzo,Ghosh:2021roe}.
A model trained this way can in principle learn the correct function across the full range of systematic variations, and the uncertainty on the output can be assessed simply by varying the conditioning parameter.
This is the optimal strategy when the form of the domain shift is known and can be parametrized, but it requires that the nuisance parameters be identified and that appropriate training samples be available across their range.
