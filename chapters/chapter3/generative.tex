
\section{Generative Models}
\label{sec:generative}

The previous section introduced several neural network architectures using binary cross-entropy as the representative loss function.
Neural networks are, however, universal function approximators, and the choice of loss function determines what they learn to compute.
This section covers generative modeling, which falls under the umbrella of unsupervised learning.
The goal is to model the probability density $p_\text{data}(x)$ from which a training dataset is drawn.
A well-trained generative model can be used to draw new samples from this distribution, evaluate the likelihood of unseen data points, or learn a compressed latent representation of the data.
Additionally generative models can be \textit{conditional} on additional information, such as a set of labels or a text prompt.
These models seek to learn the joint distribution $p_\text{data}(x, y)$ of the data and the additional information.

Generative modeling has a long history predating the deep learning era.
In what follows I will cover three specific generative models of the post-AlexNet era which have seen significant application in HEP.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generative Adversarial Networks}
\label{sec:gan}

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)~\cite{Goodfellow:2014gan} were introduced in 2014.
GANs are unique since they do not try to explicity model a likelihood of the training data.
Instead they set up an adversarial game between two networks: a \textit{generator} $G_\phi(z)$ that maps a noise vector $z \sim p(z)$ (typically a standard Gaussian) to a sample in data space, and a \textit{discriminator} $D_\psi(x) \in (0,1)$ that outputs the probability that its input is a real data point rather than a generator sample.

The two networks are trained simultaneously according to the minimax objective
\begin{equation}
    \min_\phi \max_\psi \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}}\!\left[\log D_\psi(x)\right]
    + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}\!\left[\log\!\left(1 - D_\psi(G_\phi(z))\right)\right].
    \label{eqn:gan}
\end{equation}
The discriminator is trained to distinguish real from generated samples, while the generator is trained to produce samples that fool the discriminator.
Asuming the game is ``well balanced'' and the generator has sufficient capacity, the generator distribution will eventually match $p_\text{data}$ and the discriminator can do no better than random guessing.
There is no explicit likelihood in \Cref{eqn:gan}.
The generator is never required to assign a density to the data, only to produce samples that are indistinguishable from it, so GANs are not capable of density estimation for example.
GANs are conceptually simple and capable of producing high-quality samples, but the training is notoriously difficult.
Common issues include mode collapse, where the generator learns to produce only a limited subset of the data distribution, and non-convergence, where the generator and discriminator cycle but never converge to a stable equilibrium.

GANs were among the first generative architectures applied to HEP data.
Early work demonstrated that they could generate realistic jets and calorimeter showers~\cite{Paganini:2017hrr,Paganini:2017dwg,deOliveira:2017pjk,Butter:2020qhk,Buhmann:2023pmh}, perform unfolding~\cite{Bellagente:2019uyp}, and GAN-based fast calorimeter simulation tools are still in active use within ATLAS today~\cite{ATLAS:2021pzo}.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders}
\label{sec:vae}

In contrast to GANs, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)~\cite{Kingma:2013vae} attempt to explicitly model the likelihood of the training data.
Given this approach is important to the unfolding work in \Cref{ch:unfolding}, it is worth reviewing the basics of VAEs here.
The starting point is a \textit{latent variable model}.
The assumption is that the generation of each data point $x$ is governed by a latent variable $z \sim p(z)$ from a prior (typically a Gaussian) and a conditional likelihood $p(x | z)$\footnote{The choice to introduce a latent vector $z$ relates to the manifold hypothesis discussed in \cref{sec:id}. This latent vector is typically much lower dimensional than the data representation, implying that the manifold the data cluster on can be modeled using these latent variables.}.
The complicated object is the conditional likelihood, which we attempt to model with a generative model, $p_{\theta}(x | z)$ with parameters $\theta$.
The marginal likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x | z)\, p(z)\, dz.
    \label{eqn:marginal}
\end{equation}
Direct maximum likelihood training would require evaluating this integral using an MCMC estimator for every mini-batch of data, which is intractable for high-dimensional $z$.
Attempting to model the posterior instead would produce the same issue, since by Bayes rule the posterior is inversely propertional to $p(x)$ which is the exact density we seek to model to begin with.
The VAE sidesteps this intractable posterior by introducing an \textit{encoder} network $q_\phi(z | x)$ that approximates the true posterior $p_\theta(z | x)$, and then optimizing a lower bound on the log-likelihood.

\subsubsection{Derivation of the ELBO}

Starting from the log-marginal-likelihood of a single data point $x$, we introduce $q_\phi(z|x)$ by multiplying and dividing inside the expectation:
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    &= \log \int p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)\,dz \notag \\
    &= \log \int q_\phi(z|x) \frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\,dz \notag \\
    &= \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right].
    \label{eqn:elbo_step1}
\end{align}
Applying Jensen's inequality (the log function is concave, so $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$) gives
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    &\geq \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right] \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x|z)\right]
       - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{p(z)}\right] \notag \\
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x|z)\right]}_{\text{reconstruction}}
       - \underbrace{D_\text{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\right)}_{\text{regularization}}.
    \label{eqn:elbo}
\end{align}
This lower bound is called the \textit{Evidence Lower Bound} (ELBO).
The ELBO can be maximized with respect to both $\theta$ and $\phi$, pushing the density provided by the decoder $p_{\theta}(x | z)$ toward the true log-likelihood.
The gap between the ELBO and $\log p_\theta(x)$ is exactly the KL divergence between the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$ and the true posterior $p_\theta(z|x)$, so the ELBO is tight when the encoder perfectly recovers the true posterior.

The ELBO has a natural two-term interpretation.
The \textit{reconstruction term} $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]$ encourages the decoder to accurately reconstruct $x$ from samples from the latent distribution.
The \textit{regularization term} $D_\text{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))$ penalizes the encoder for producing a posterior that deviates from the prior, encouraging the latent space to remain well-structured and preventing the model from simply memorizing the training data.
Dropping the KL term reduces the VAE to a standard autoencoder, which can reconstruct training data but whose latent space lacks a statistical interpretation, making sampling difficult.

In the standard VAE, both the prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\phi(x)))$ are chosen to be Gaussian.
Under this choice the KL divergence has a closed form.
For a $d$-dimensional latent space,
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{KL}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\right)
    = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\right),
    \label{eqn:kl_gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ are the $j$-th components of the encoder's predicted mean and standard deviation.
This term can be computed analytically and differentiated without any Monte Carlo estimation.

\subsubsection{The Reparameterization Trick}

The reconstruction term in \Cref{eqn:elbo} requires differentiating through a sampling operation $z \sim q_\phi(z|x)$, which is not directly differentiable with respect to $\phi$.
The \textit{reparameterization trick} resolves this by rewriting the sample as a deterministic function of the encoder output and a fixed noise variable:
\begin{equation}
    z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
    \label{eqn:reparam}
\end{equation}
The randomness is now isolated in $\varepsilon$, which does not depend on $\phi$, so gradients flow through $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$ to the encoder parameters without difficulty.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction Loss and Limitations}

For continuous data, the decoder likelihood $p_\theta(x|z)$ is typically chosen to be a Gaussian, $p_\theta(x|z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I)$, where all neural network parameters serve only to calculate the mean $\mu_{\theta}(z)$.
This choice reduces the reconstruction term to a mean squared error (MSE) loss between the input and the decoder's predicted mean $\mu_\theta(z)$.
The full ELBO training objective is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{VAE}(\theta, \phi; x) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left\|x - \mu_\theta(z)\right\|^2
    + D_\text{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\right),
    \label{eqn:vae_loss}
\end{equation}
minimized by gradient descent over mini-batches of training examples, with $z$ computed via the reparameterization trick at each step.
The overall encoder-decoder structure is illustrated in \Cref{fig:vae}.

One consequence of the assumed Gaussian likelihood is worth noting explicitly.
The MSE reconstruction loss trains the decoder to predict the mean of $p_\theta(x|z)$, which is the optimal prediction under squared error.
If $p_\text{data}(x|z)$ is multimodal, the decoder's predicted mean will be a weighted average across modes rather than a sample from any of them.
In practice this can result in poor sample quality.
More broadly, any observable that depends on the shape of $p(x|z)$ rather than its mean will be poorly captured.
This limitation becomes particularly relevant for the unfolding application in \Cref{ch:unfolding}.

Despite these limitations, VAEs are substantially easier to train than GANs and were adopted within HEP for generative tasks including fast simulation and anomaly detection~\cite{Howard:2021pos,Tsan:2021brw,Cheng:2020dal}.

\subsection{Normalizing Flows}
\label{sec:flows}

In contrast to VAEs, normalizing flows model the likelihood of the data directly, rather than using an auxiliary network to approximate the posterior.
They handle the intractable integral in \Cref{eqn:marginal} by insisting that the modeled likelihood is \textit{invertible}, making the integral over $z$ trivial to compute because only one $z$ maps to a particular $x$.
More specifically, define a \textit{bijective} mapping $f_\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}$ between data space $\mathcal{X}$ and a latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ of equal dimension, such that the mapping and its inverse are both computable in closed form.
Given a simple prior $p(z)$ (again typically a standard Gaussian) and this bijective mapping, the change-of-variables formula gives an \textit{exact} expression for the data likelihood:
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x) = \log p(f_\theta(x)) + \log\left|\det J_{f_\theta}(x)\right|,
    \label{eqn:flow_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $J_{f_\theta}(x) = \partial f_\theta / \partial x$ is the Jacobian of the transformation.
Training maximizes this log-likelihood over the training data, with no approximation and no auxiliary network.
A key consequence is that once trained, the flow model supports not just sampling (by drawing $z \sim p(z)$ and computing $f_\theta^{-1}(z)$) but also exact density evaluation of any point $x$.
This ability to evaluate $p_\theta(x)$ without sampling is a unique ability of flow models.

The central engineering challenge is constructing a bijective $f_\theta$ whose Jacobian determinant is tractable to compute.
For a general MLP layer the Jacobian is an $n \times n$ matrix and its determinant costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$, which is prohibitive for high-dimensional $x$.
\textit{Coupling layers}~\cite{dinh2016density} address this by constraining their Jacobian to be triangular, making the determinant simply the product of the diagonal entries which is cheap to compute.

In a coupling layer the input $x$ is split into two halves, $x_{1:k}$ and $x_{k+1:n}$.
The first half passes through unchanged, while the second half undergoes an elementwise transformation whose parameters are determined by the first half.
For example the \textit{affine coupling layer}~\cite{dinh2016density} applies the transformation
\begin{align}
    z_{1:k} &= x_{1:k}, \label{eqn:coupling_id} \\
    z_{k+1:n} &= x_{k+1:n} \odot \exp\!\left(s_\theta(x_{1:k})\right) + t_\theta(x_{1:k}),
    \label{eqn:coupling_affine}
\end{align}
where $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$ are arbitrary neural networks (typically MLPs) that act only on the unchanged half.
Because $z_{k+1:n}$ is an elementwise function of $x_{k+1:n}$ given $x_{1:k}$, the Jacobian is lower triangular and its log-determinant is simply $\sum_i s_\theta(x_{1:k})_i$.
By alternating the partition between layers so that $x_{1:k}$ is transformed in the next layer, the full input can be transformed after a few coupling layers.
This construction is illustrated in \Cref{fig:coupling_flow}.

The affine transformation in \Cref{eqn:coupling_affine} is simple and computationally efficient but relatively limited in expressiveness.
State-of-the-art flow models replace the affine map with a monotone \textit{rational-quadratic spline} (RQS)~\cite{Durkan:2019nsf}, which parameterizes a piecewise rational function whose knot positions and derivatives are output by $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$.
RQS coupling layers are the current standard in most flow-based models applied to HEP data.

Normalizing flows are a niche topic in the broader deep learning community.
The requirement that $f_\theta$ be bijective forces the latent and data spaces to have the same dimension, preventing the dimensionality reduction that makes VAEs and other latent variable models useful for representation learning.
This architectural constraint also limits scalability to very high-dimensional data.
Hpwever HEP data are relatively low-dimensional.
Jet and event data typically involve tens to a few hundred dimensions rather than the millions of pixels in natural images, and flow models perform very well on these medium dimensional tasks.
Normalizing flows have been applied across a broad range of HEP tasks, including fast detector simulation~\cite{Krause:2021ilc}, unfolding~\cite{Bellagente:2020piv}, and anomaly detection~\cite{Hallin:2021wme}.
They have only recently been displaced as the go-to generative model for HEP applications by diffusion and flow-matching models, which I'll turn to next.
