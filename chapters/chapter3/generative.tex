
\section{Generative Models}
\label{sec:generative}

The previous section introduced several neural network architectures and their application to supervised classification tasks, using binary cross-entropy as the representative loss function.
Neural networks are, however, universal function approximators, and the choice of loss function determines what they learn to compute.
This section covers generative modeling, a prime example of unsupervised learning, in which the goal is not to predict a label but to model the probability density $p_\text{data}(x)$ from which a training dataset is drawn.
A well-trained generative model can be used to draw new samples from this distribution, evaluate the likelihood of unseen data points, or learn a compressed latent representation of the data.
All three capabilities appear at various points in this thesis.

Generative modeling has a long history predating the deep learning era, but the following three architectures, which emerged in the years following AlexNet, define the landscape of modern deep generative models.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generative Adversarial Networks}
\label{sec:gan}

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)~\cite{Goodfellow:2014gan} were introduced in 2014 and represented a conceptual departure from prior generative approaches.
Rather than defining a tractable likelihood and maximizing it, a GAN sets up an adversarial game between two networks: a \textit{generator} $G_\phi(z)$ that maps a noise vector $z \sim p(z)$ (typically a standard Gaussian) to a sample in data space, and a \textit{discriminator} $D_\psi(x) \in (0,1)$ that outputs the probability that its input is a real data point rather than a generator sample.

The two networks are trained simultaneously according to the minimax objective
\begin{equation}
    \min_\phi \max_\psi \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}}\!\left[\log D_\psi(x)\right]
    + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}\!\left[\log\!\left(1 - D_\psi(G_\phi(z))\right)\right].
    \label{eqn:gan}
\end{equation}
The discriminator is trained to distinguish real from generated samples (the $\max$ step), while the generator is trained to produce samples that fool the discriminator (the $\min$ step).
At the Nash equilibrium of this game, the generator distribution matches $p_\text{data}$ exactly and the discriminator can do no better than random guessing.
Crucially, there is no explicit likelihood in \Cref{eqn:gan}: the generator is never required to assign a density to the data, only to produce samples that are indistinguishable from it.
This makes GANs conceptually simple and capable of producing high-quality samples, but the adversarial training dynamic is notoriously unstable.
Common failure modes include mode collapse, where the generator learns to produce only a limited subset of the data distribution, and training divergence.

GANs were among the first generative architectures applied to HEP data.
Early work demonstrated that they could generate realistic jet images and calorimeter showers~\cite{Paganini:2017hrr,Paganini:2017dwg,deOliveira:2017pjk}, and GAN-based fast calorimeter simulation tools are still in active use within ATLAS today~\cite{ATLAS:2023tgo}.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders}
\label{sec:vae}

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)~\cite{Kingma:2013vae} take a probabilistic approach to generative modeling.
The starting point is a \textit{latent variable model}: we posit that each observed data point $x$ was generated by first drawing a latent variable $z \sim p(z)$ from a prior (typically a standard Gaussian) and then drawing $x$ from a conditional likelihood $p_\theta(x | z)$ parameterized by a decoder network.
The marginal likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x | z)\, p(z)\, dz.
    \label{eqn:marginal}
\end{equation}
Direct maximum likelihood training would require evaluating this integral, which is intractable for high-dimensional $z$.
The VAE sidesteps this by introducing an \textit{encoder} network $q_\phi(z | x)$ that approximates the true posterior $p_\theta(z | x)$, and then optimizing a lower bound on the log-likelihood.

\subsubsection{Derivation of the ELBO}

Starting from the log-marginal-likelihood of a single data point $x$, we introduce $q_\phi(z|x)$ by multiplying and dividing inside the expectation:
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    &= \log \int p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)\,dz \notag \\
    &= \log \int q_\phi(z|x) \frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\,dz \notag \\
    &= \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right].
    \label{eqn:elbo_step1}
\end{align}
Applying Jensen's inequality (the log function is concave, so $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$) gives
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    &\geq \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x|z)\,p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right] \notag \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x|z)\right]
       - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{p(z)}\right] \notag \\
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x|z)\right]}_{\text{reconstruction}}
       - \underbrace{D_\text{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\right)}_{\text{regularization}}.
    \label{eqn:elbo}
\end{align}
This lower bound is called the \textit{Evidence Lower Bound} (ELBO).
Maximizing the ELBO with respect to both $\theta$ and $\phi$ simultaneously tightens the bound from below toward the true log-likelihood.
The gap between the ELBO and $\log p_\theta(x)$ is exactly the KL divergence between the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$ and the true posterior $p_\theta(z|x)$, so the ELBO is tight when the encoder perfectly recovers the true posterior.

The ELBO has a natural two-term interpretation.
The \textit{reconstruction term} $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]$ encourages the decoder to accurately reconstruct $x$ from samples of the latent code produced by the encoder.
The \textit{regularization term} $D_\text{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))$ penalizes the encoder for producing a posterior that deviates from the prior, encouraging the latent space to remain well-structured and preventing the model from simply memorizing the training data.
Dropping the KL term reduces the VAE to a standard autoencoder, which can reconstruct training data but whose latent space lacks the regularity needed to sample from.

In the standard VAE, both the prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\phi(x)))$ are chosen to be Gaussian.
Under this choice the KL divergence has a closed form.
For a $d$-dimensional latent space,
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{KL}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\right)
    = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\right),
    \label{eqn:kl_gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ are the $j$-th components of the encoder's predicted mean and standard deviation.
This term can therefore be computed analytically and differentiated without any Monte Carlo estimation.

\subsubsection{The Reparameterization Trick}

The reconstruction term in \Cref{eqn:elbo} requires differentiating through a sampling operation $z \sim q_\phi(z|x)$, which is not directly differentiable with respect to $\phi$.
The \textit{reparameterization trick} resolves this by rewriting the sample as a deterministic function of the encoder output and a fixed noise variable:
\begin{equation}
    z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
    \label{eqn:reparam}
\end{equation}
The randomness is now isolated in $\varepsilon$, which does not depend on $\phi$, so gradients flow through $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$ to the encoder parameters without difficulty.
This simple manipulation is what makes end-to-end training of the VAE possible.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction Loss and Limitations}

For continuous data, the decoder likelihood $p_\theta(x|z)$ is typically chosen to be a Gaussian, $p_\theta(x|z) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I)$, which reduces the reconstruction term to a mean squared error (MSE) loss between the input and the decoder's predicted mean $\mu_\theta(z)$.
The full ELBO training objective is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{VAE}(\theta, \phi; x) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left\|x - \mu_\theta(z)\right\|^2
    + D_\text{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\right),
    \label{eqn:vae_loss}
\end{equation}
minimized by gradient descent over mini-batches of training examples, with $z$ computed via the reparameterization trick at each step.
The overall encoder-decoder structure is illustrated in \Cref{fig:vae}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/ch3/vae_diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the variational autoencoder architecture. The encoder $q_\phi(z|x)$ maps an input $x$ to a distribution over latent codes $z$. A sample is drawn via the reparameterization trick and passed to the decoder $p_\theta(x|z)$, which reconstructs $x$. Training maximizes the ELBO, balancing reconstruction quality against regularization of the latent space toward the prior $p(z)$.}
    \label{fig:vae}
\end{figure}

One consequence of the Gaussian decoder is worth noting explicitly.
The MSE reconstruction loss trains the decoder to predict the mean of $p_\theta(x|z)$, which is the optimal prediction under squared error.
If $p_\text{data}(x|z)$ is multimodal, the decoder's predicted mean will be a weighted average across modes rather than a sample from any of them.
In practice this manifests as blurry reconstructions in image generation tasks.
More broadly, any observable that depends on the shape of $p(x|z)$ rather than its mean will be poorly captured.
This limitation becomes particularly relevant for the unfolding application in \Cref{ch:unfolding}, where capturing the full shape of the inferred distribution is essential.

Despite these limitations, VAEs are substantially easier to train than GANs and were adopted quickly within HEP for generative tasks including fast calorimeter simulation, anomaly detection, and density estimation~\cite{Howard:2021pos,Cerri:2018anq,Butter:2019cae}.

\subsection{Normalizing Flows}
\label{sec:flows}

Normalizing flows take a different approach to generative modeling that avoids the approximations inherent in both the GAN adversarial game and the VAE's variational lower bound.
A normalizing flow defines a \textit{bijective} mapping $f_\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}$ between data space $\mathcal{X}$ and a latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ of equal dimension, such that the mapping and its inverse are both computable in closed form.
Given a simple prior $p(z)$ (again typically a standard Gaussian), the change-of-variables formula gives an \textit{exact} expression for the data likelihood:
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x) = \log p(f_\theta(x)) + \log\left|\det J_{f_\theta}(x)\right|,
    \label{eqn:flow_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $J_{f_\theta}(x) = \partial f_\theta / \partial x$ is the Jacobian of the transformation.
Training maximizes this exact log-likelihood over the training data, with no approximation and no auxiliary network.
A key consequence is that once trained, the flow model supports not just sampling (by drawing $z \sim p(z)$ and computing $f_\theta^{-1}(z)$) but also exact density evaluation of any point $x$.
This ability to evaluate $p_\theta(x)$ without sampling is a significant practical advantage in applications such as reweighting and anomaly detection, where likelihood ratios are needed directly.

The central engineering challenge is constructing a bijective $f_\theta$ whose Jacobian determinant is tractable to compute.
For a general network the Jacobian is an $n \times n$ matrix and its determinant costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$, which is prohibitive for high-dimensional $x$.
\textit{Coupling layers}~\cite{TODO:dinh_realnvp} address this by constructing transformations with triangular Jacobians, whose determinants reduce to a product of diagonal entries.

In a coupling layer the input $x$ is split into two halves, $x_{1:k}$ and $x_{k+1:n}$.
The first half passes through unchanged, while the second half undergoes an elementwise transformation whose parameters are determined by the first half:
\begin{align}
    z_{1:k} &= x_{1:k}, \label{eqn:coupling_id} \\
    z_{k+1:n} &= x_{k+1:n} \odot \exp\!\left(s_\theta(x_{1:k})\right) + t_\theta(x_{1:k}),
    \label{eqn:coupling_affine}
\end{align}
where $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$ are arbitrary neural networks (scale and translation functions) that act only on the unchanged half.
This is an \textit{affine coupling layer}~\cite{TODO:dinh_realnvp}.
Because $z_{k+1:n}$ is an elementwise function of $x_{k+1:n}$ given $x_{1:k}$, the Jacobian is lower triangular and its log-determinant is simply $\sum_i s_\theta(x_{1:k})_i$.
The transformation is invertible for any choice of $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$, so these networks can be arbitrarily expressive without any constraints.
By alternating the partition between layers (so that $x_{1:k}$ is transformed in the next layer), the full input can be transformed after a few coupling layers.
This construction is illustrated in \Cref{fig:coupling_flow}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/ch3/coupling_flow_diagram.png}
    \caption{Illustration of a normalizing flow built from coupling layers. At each layer the input is split into two halves; one half determines the transformation applied to the other. Alternating the partition across layers ensures all dimensions are transformed. Figure from~\cite{Albergo:2021bna} under Creative Commons license.}
    \label{fig:coupling_flow}
\end{figure}

The affine transformation in \Cref{eqn:coupling_affine} is simple and computationally efficient but relatively limited in expressiveness.
More expressive alternatives replace the affine map with a monotone \textit{rational-quadratic spline} (RQS)~\cite{Durkan:2019nsf}, which parameterizes a piecewise rational function whose knot positions and derivatives are output by $s_\theta$ and $t_\theta$.
RQS coupling layers are the current standard in most flow-based models applied to HEP data.

Normalizing flows occupy a somewhat niche position in the broader deep learning community.
The requirement that $f_\theta$ be bijective forces the latent and data spaces to have the same dimension, preventing the kind of dimensionality reduction that makes VAEs and other latent variable models useful for representation learning.
This architectural constraint also limits scalability to very high-dimensional data.
In HEP, however, the relevant data spaces are comparatively low-dimensional: jet observables, event-level features, and unfolding targets typically involve tens to a few hundred dimensions rather than the millions of pixels in natural images.
Within this regime, the exact likelihood evaluation that flows provide is a decisive advantage.
Normalizing flows have been applied across a broad range of HEP tasks, including fast detector simulation~\cite{Krause:2021ilc}, unbinned reweighting~\cite{Bellagente:2020piv}, and anomaly detection~\cite{Hallin:2021wme}.

\subsection{Towards Diffusion Models}

GANs, VAEs, and normalizing flows each offer a different tradeoff between sample quality, training stability, and tractability.
GANs produce sharp samples but are unstable to train and provide no density estimate.
VAEs are stable and provide a structured latent space, but the Gaussian decoder introduces a systematic bias toward blurry mean predictions.
Normalizing flows offer exact likelihoods but are architecturally constrained by the bijectivity requirement and do not scale as favorably to high dimensions.

Diffusion models, introduced in \Cref{sec:diffusion}, sidestep these constraints by framing generation as an iterative denoising process rather than a single-pass transformation.
They combine the training stability and likelihood tractability of VAEs with sample quality that matches or exceeds GANs, at the cost of requiring many sequential forward passes at inference time.
Their success in both the broader AI community and in HEP has made them the current method of choice for high-fidelity generative modeling tasks, including the unfolding work at the center of this thesis.
