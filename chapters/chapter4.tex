
\chapter{Jet Classification at the LHC}
\label{ch:tagging}

Jet classification, commonly referred to as ``jet tagging'', was one of the first applications of deep learning in particle physics and remains arguably the most important.
The techniques covered in this chapter are used by both ATLAS and CMS and are driving improvements in the search for di-Higgs production.
This chapter will introduce jet tagging as a problem, then cover an ATLAS study on top tagging that is the first original research contribution in this thesis.
It will close with a discussion of how jet tagging has progressed since the publication of this paper and end with some prospects for the high luminosity LHC.

\section{Jet Tagging}
\label{sec:tagging-intro}

Most searches and measurements at the LHC are concerned with the high-energy scattering of partons.
In principle, one would like to directly measure the identity and kinematics of the individual quarks and gluons produced in these collisions.
In practice, the parton shower and hadronization processes make this impossible: the colored partons produced in the hard scatter are never directly observable.
Instead, the ATLAS and CMS detectors record jets, collimated sprays of hadrons that are reconstructed and used as proxies for the initiating partons.
Inferring the kinematics of the originating parton from a jet is straightforward in principle: one sums the four-momenta of the jet's constituent hadrons.
Inferring the \emph{type} of parton that initiated the jet is the problem of jet tagging.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/ch4/jet_types_diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic illustration of the two broad categories of jets produced in LHC collisions. Boosted jets (left) arise when a massive particle such as a top quark, $W/Z$ boson, or Higgs boson decays hadronically with high transverse momentum, causing its daughter quarks to merge into a single large-radius jet with non-trivial internal substructure. Flavor-tagged jets (right) arise from the production of heavy-flavor quarks, whose long-lived $B$ and $D$ hadron decay products leave a displaced secondary vertex inside the jet.}
    \label{fig:jet-types}
\end{figure}

Historically, two distinct jet tagging problems have been studied at the LHC, though modern deep learning methods have largely unified them.
The first is \textit{boosted jet tagging}.
When a massive particle such as a top quark, $W$ or $Z$ boson, or Higgs boson is produced with a transverse momentum much larger than its mass ($\pt \gg m$), its hadronic decay products are emitted nearly collinearly.
The resulting jets from each daughter quark merge into a single large-radius jet with non-trivial substructure: a two-pronged substructure for $W/Z/H \to q\bar{q}$ decays, or a three-pronged substructure for $t \to Wbq\bar{q}$ decays.
Identifying these boosted jets against the overwhelming background of high-\pt light-quark and gluon jets requires exploiting this substructure.
Early methods for doing so relied on multivariate discriminants such as BDTs or shallow neural networks trained on a set of hand-engineered substructure observables~\cite{Altheimer:2013yza, Thaler:2008ju}.

The second category is \textit{flavor tagging}.
When a bottom or charm quark is produced in the hard scatter, the resulting jet contains a $B$ or $D$ hadron.
These hadrons are long-lived relative to the light hadrons produced in light-quark and gluon jets, and they therefore travel a measurable distance before decaying, leaving a displaced secondary vertex inside the jet.
Identifying jets that contain $B$ or $D$ hadrons as opposed to jets from light quarks or gluons is called $b$- or $c$-tagging.
As in the boosted case, early flavor taggers reconstructed the displaced vertex explicitly and used this information as an input to a BDT or neural network~\cite{ATLAS:2016gsw}.

The two problems are illustrated schematically in \Cref{fig:jet-types}.
Both are fundamentally classification tasks: one must distinguish a signal jet from a background jet using the information available in the detector.
The primary distinction between them is the nature of the discriminating information.
Boosted tagging relies on the global spatial structure of energy deposits within a large-radius jet, while flavor tagging relies on precise tracking information near the jet core.
In both cases, the task is made difficult by the complexity of the QCD background and the large dynamic range of the relevant kinematic variables.

The application of deep learning to both of these problems has been transformative.
The first papers demonstrating the use of convolutional neural networks for jet classification appeared in 2014--2015, only two or three years after the AlexNet moment~\cite{Cogan:2014oua}.
The key insight was that a jet could be represented as an image: binning the constituent four-momenta on a grid in the $(\eta, \phi)$ plane and treating the resulting pixelated energy deposits as an image amenable to convolutional processing.
Rather than relying on any expert-designed features, these networks learned directly from the raw detector information which patterns discriminate signal from background.
This end-to-end approach consistently outperformed the hand-engineered methods that preceded it, and it set the template for subsequent improvements.

The section that follows describes in detail a particular boosted jet tagging task: identifying jets initiated by top quarks against the background of light-quark and gluon jets.
Boosted top tagging is the prototypical multi-prong jet tagging problem and was the subject of many early deep learning applications in particle physics.
It remains the canonical benchmark for new tagging methods.
The study presented here, carried out in 2022--2023, constitutes the first original research contribution of this thesis.
The lessons drawn from the binary top-versus-QCD classification problem remain relevant to the multi-class taggers of today, to which I will return in \Cref{sec:tagging-future}.
