
\chapter{Unfolding}
\label{ch:unfolding}

This Chapter is a detailed treatment of \textit{unfolding}, a problem in particle physics data analysis that has been a primary focus of my Ph.D. research.
It will introduce the problem and why it is important in LHC physics, then illustrate conventional solutions that have been in use for many years.
Then it will turn to AI/ML based unfolding methods, highlighting a few methods that I played a role in developing.
These methods enable very high dimensional cross section measurements with many downstream physics applications.
The Chapter closes with an overview of the \Omnifold method.
This method is applied to experimental data to produce such a high dimensional cross section measurement in \Cref{ch:zjets}.

\section{Introduction to Unfolding}
\label{sec:unfolding-intro}

The detectors used in particle physics experiments are imperfect instruments.
They introduce non-trivial distortions into every observable they record, and precise measurements must account for these distortions.
There is one important exception: when a measurement is performed in sufficiently coarse bins such that the bin widths are much larger than the detector resolution, the detector response matrix (see \Cref{sec:binned-unfolding-methods}) is nearly diagonal and its effect can safely be neglected.
Outside of this regime, two broad strategies have emerged for treating the detector response.

The first is \textit{forward folding}.
In this approach, theoretical predictions are expressed as fully exclusive particle-level predictions, specifying for example the four-momenta of all long-lived particles that interact with the detector.
These predictions are then passed through a simulation of the detector response and compared to the raw experimental data in detector space.
The second strategy is \textit{unfolding}.
Rather than folding theory into detector space, statistical methods are used to construct an approximate inversion of the detector response, which is then applied to the data~\cite{Cowan:2002in}.
The result is a measurement at the particle level that can be compared directly to theoretical predictions without any further reference to the detector simulation.
Both approaches require access to an accurate detector simulation, which underpins either the forward-folded theory prediction or the response inversion.

The two strategies carry distinct advantages and disadvantages.
Forward folding is strictly more precise.
Because unfolding inverts the detector simulation, the best case is that the detector response is perfectly invertible and no additional uncertainty is introduced; in practice this is rarely true.
Despite this limitation, unfolding is often worth the added uncertainty.
Unfolded measurements can be compared directly to any theoretical prediction, including exclusive calculations such as resummed predictions of jet substructure observables, without requiring access to the detector simulation.
This is a significant practical benefit when the simulation is expensive to run, which is typically the case at ATLAS and CMS, or when it is unavailable for legacy datasets.
In contrast, forward folding requires the simulation to be re-run for each new theoretical prediction, is difficult to combine across analyses using different detector responses, and is not straightforwardly applicable to non-inclusive theory calculations.

Whether a given analysis uses forward folding or unfolding depends heavily on context.
Searches for beyond-Standard-Model (BSM) particles almost never unfold.
Such searches look for coherent shapes in low-statistics regions of phase space, and unfolding would substantially reduce sensitivity; there is also little practical motivation since well-established procedures exist within the experimental collaborations for re-interpreting searches via forward folding.
Among Standard Model measurements, unfolding is used in roughly one third of published ATLAS and CMS results, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:paper-citations}.
Extractions of SM parameters such as the top quark or Higgs boson masses typically do not unfold, while differential cross section measurements typically do.
This picture is complicated by the fact that some unfolded measurements are not described as such explicitly.
The Simplified Template Cross Section (STXS) framework used in ATLAS and CMS Higgs measurements, for example, is an instance of profile likelihood unfolding (see \Cref{sec:binned-unfolding-methods}), but is rarely labeled as such in publications~\cite{Berger:2019wnu}.
In summary, unfolding occupies a niche but important role in the LHC physics program, and is essential for data preservation and legacy comparisons to theory.
